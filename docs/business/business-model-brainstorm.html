1 · The trigger

The article that started this: the "harness shift"

The Next Web, "Cursor price hikes push enterprises to Claude Code, and beyond" (Jul 2026).

Three moves the piece describes:

  • The repricing shock. Cursor moved most customers to usage-based billing; renewals now land hard. One IT consultancy paid ~$200k for 800 licences one year, then was quoted ~$1.5m for the same usage at renewal.
  • The near-term flight to Claude Code. Enterprises are shifting spend to Anthropic's model-aligned harness. (Notably, routing-firm Weave's data shows Claude Code bills actually rose more than Cursor's over six months — so this is not purely a cost story.)
  • The real story — the harness shift. Savvier customers route around both, swapping proprietary tools for open-source harnesses over cheaper / local models, cutting AI-coding bills by as much as 97%.
The thesis in one line

"The most expensive part of AI coding is no longer the model. It is the software wrapped around it." Value — and pricing power — is migrating from the model to the harness. But the same open-source wave is commoditizing the harness itself.

2 · Positioning

GlassCoder is already built for this world

Per its own design docs, GlassCoder is "the harness and measurement half" of the capability equation — a local-first coding agent that drives any model over an OpenAI-compatible seam. It does no training and no tensor math; everything lives above the HTTP boundary.

capability ≈ model × harness × context

So the strategic question is not "can GlassCoder ride the wave" — it clearly can. It is: which part of the harness layer stays defensible once the loop itself is a commodity? GlassCoder's own docs say the controller loop is deliberately ~40 lines and legible — i.e. easy to copy. The value is therefore not in the loop. It is in the three first-class pillars most harnesses lack: robust logging/transparency, transparent code changes, and performance measurement.

3 · Moats

What is genuinely defensible

Moat 1

Measurement / the Lab

Ablation arms, pass@1, cost-per-solved-task, tokens-to-solve, JSONL metrics, cross-run charts. The proof engine for the 97% claim — rare, hard to copy quickly.

Moat 2

Sovereignty / local-first

Source never crosses the network seam; runs on your own GPUs (the DGX Spark setup docs are telling). The buy-reason for firms that legally cannot use cloud coding tools.

Moat 3

Transparency / audit

Every edit is a reviewable diff; every run a reconstructable transcript; secrets redacted. An AI agent you can put in a regulated codebase and still pass an audit.

Moat 4

.NET-native

Roslyn in-process diagnostics, MSBuild, C#. Almost every rival is JS/Python-first. The conservative on-prem buyers who want this live in Microsoft. A niche, not a weakness.

5 · Recommendation

Recommended path

Recommendation

Sell the outcome, not the software: "cut your AI-coding bill without cutting quality, on hardware you control, in a way you can audit."

Package that as open-core + a sovereign on-prem license, with the Lab as the paid differentiator that proves the savings are safe. Lead with paid services to land two or three regulated .NET lighthouse customers — each engagement produces an ablation report that doubles as the product spec and the sales collateral.

  1. Services first (next ~2 quarters). Paid "cut your Claude/Cursor bill" pilots. Revenue now, and every pilot yields proof.
  2. Productize the Lab. It is the one thing rivals can't cheaply copy and the thing that de-risks the downshift for a buyer.
  3. Open-core the loop, sell the enterprise layer. Governance, fleet, air-gap, and support on top of a free, copyable core.

6 · Anti-pattern

The trap to avoid

Do not

Do not price GlassCoder as "a cheaper Cursor" — a per-seat licence on the agent loop. That is exactly the layer the article says is collapsing to zero, and GlassCoder's own docs make the loop small and open by design. You cannot out-cheap free, and per-seat pricing on a commodity invites the same renewal-shock backlash that is currently pushing customers off Cursor.

Charge instead for the layers that rise in value as the harness commoditizes: proof (the Lab), sovereignty (on-prem / .NET), and governance (audit & fleet). Chasing solo devs and cloud-native JS/Python startups is the same trap in disguise — they will use free harnesses over hosted APIs and never pay you.

7 · Value

Value proposition, in plain terms

"Proof, sovereignty, governance" is jargon until you say what each does for a buyer:

Proof — "Know before you switch."

Everyone wants the 97% savings, but nobody can safely act on it because they can't answer: if I move from Claude to a local model, do I lose quality? The Lab turns that from a leap of faith into a number — same task suite, both models, pass@1 and cost-per-solved-task side by side. You make a six-figure switching decision on data, and the ablation report is the receipt for your CFO.

Sovereignty — "Your code never leaves the building."

Cursor and Claude Code send your source to someone else's cloud; for a whole class of company that is a hard legal no. GlassCoder runs the agent against models on your own hardware — the source never crosses the seam. You get an AI coding agent you are actually allowed to use.

Governance — "Every change is auditable and gated."

Every proposed edit is a diff you approve before it lands; every run is a reconstructable transcript; secrets are redacted. You can put an AI agent into a regulated codebase and still pass an audit — prove exactly what changed, why, and who approved it.

Pitch line

"The AI coding agent regulated .NET teams are allowed to run on their own hardware — and can prove is cheaper without being worse."

8 · Buyer

Target customer

Common thread: real code, real compliance exposure, real Microsoft stacks, and either a cloud-AI bill or a cloud-AI ban.

Primary ICP — regulated .NET enterprises

  • Banks, insurers, capital-markets firms — large C#/.NET estates, strict data-residency rules, already feeling the renewal shock.
  • Defense, government, and gov-adjacent contractors — often air-gapped by mandate; cloud coding tools are simply off the table. Sovereignty is the entire buying criterion.
  • Healthcare / pharma — PHI and IP sensitivity, heavy enterprise-tier .NET.

Secondary ICP — "downshift" buyers (any stack)

  • Mid-to-large eng orgs facing a usage-based renewal spike who need the Lab to justify a move to local/cheaper models internally. Here you may sell only the measurement layer, not the whole harness.

What actually moves these numbers

  1. Logo count, not price. The entire gap between A and B is "did you land ~18 vs ~50 regulated logos." Every dollar of realism is in shortening the sales cycle — which is what the services-led, ablation-report-as-proof motion is for.
  2. The sales cycle is the tax. Two enterprise pilots slipping two quarters wipes out a year. Model cash accordingly.
  3. Services is oxygen, not the business. It funds Year 1–2 and generates proof, but it doesn't compound. The recurring on-prem line is what makes this worth more than a consultancy.

10 · Regional lens

European & Belgium context

Europe doesn't just tweak this plan — it reorders it. The moat ranked as a niche in a US framing (sovereignty) becomes the primary wedge in the EU, and Belgium is an unusually good place to start.

Verify before quoting

The regulatory specifics below (AI Act phase-in, DORA scope, grant programmes) move fast. Treat this as strategic shape, not legal advice — confirm current status before putting any of it in front of a customer.

The big shift — sovereignty goes from niche to mainstream

In a US framing, "your code never leaves the building" is mostly a defense/gov story. In the EU it is a first-order enterprise anxiety, and it is being legislated into a buying requirement:

  • GDPR + Schrems II. Sending source code (which routinely contains personal data, secrets, and trade secrets) to a US-hosted Claude/Cursor endpoint is a genuine cross-border-transfer headache. The EU–US Data Privacy Framework papers over it but is already under legal challenge. On-prem / EU-resident inference makes the problem disappear.
  • DORA (financial sector). Firms must actively manage ICT third-party concentration risk. A hard dependency on a US AI SaaS for the engineering workflow is now something a regulator expects a mitigation for — GlassCoder is the mitigation.
  • EU AI Act. Beyond the obligations themselves, it has created a culture of record-keeping and traceability. GlassCoder's "every run is a reconstructable transcript" becomes documentation infrastructure, not just a dev feature.
  • Digital-sovereignty politics. GAIA-X, sovereign-cloud mandates, and active pressure to reduce dependence on US hyperscalers. The wind is at your back in a way it is not in the US.
EU pitch line

"An AI coding agent that keeps your source in the EU, on hardware you control, with the audit trail your regulator already expects."

The fully-European sovereign stack — a real narrative

The part that is better in Europe than anywhere: you can assemble an end-to-end sovereign stack — GlassCoder (harness, .NET) + Mistral (European, open-weight models) + EU-hosted or on-prem GPUs. No US vendor anywhere in the loop. GlassCoder's model-agnostic seam (/v1, served aliases) makes this a config choice, not a rebuild. For a Belgian bank, an EU institution, or a defense-adjacent contractor, that is the reason the project gets approved.

Why Belgium is a genuine beachhead

Institutions

Brussels = EU + NATO capital

Dense concentration of public-sector IT, EU agencies, and gov-adjacent contractors — buyers for whom sovereignty is non-negotiable and budgets are real.

Finance infra

SWIFT, Euroclear, the banks

SWIFT (La Hulpe), Euroclear (Brussels), KBC, Belfius, ING/BNPP Fortis — among the most security- and regulation-obsessed shops on earth, heavily Microsoft/.NET in the enterprise tier.

Stack fit

.NET is strong here

Belgian and EU public/enterprise IT is Microsoft-heavy — the exact segment nearly every rival ignores.

Capital

Non-dilutive funding

Horizon Europe, EIC, and Flemish/Walloon regional grants (VLAIO & equivalents) can subsidize the R&D phase — de-risking Year 1 without giving up equity.

The honest downsides — don't romanticize it

  • Smaller, fragmented market. Belgium alone is tiny; the real market is "the EU" — 27 procurement regimes, multiple languages, no single sales motion.
  • Slow, tender-based procurement. Public sector and banks are slower than the 9–18 month cycle already flagged. Cash planning matters even more.
  • Lower ACVs / more price sensitivity in parts of the market — partly offset by the sovereignty premium and large public-sector deals.
  • GPU scarcity. Local inference needs local compute, and the EU is behind on GPU availability — real friction for the "run it on your own hardware" pitch, eased by the smaller-sovereign-model trend.
  • Thinner VC. European deep-tech capital is shallower than the US — another argument for the grant-funded, services-led, capital-efficient path.
